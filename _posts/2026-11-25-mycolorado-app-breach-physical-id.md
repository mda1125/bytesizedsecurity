Your phone holds your ID. This week, Colorado showed why your wallet should too.

**TL;DR:** Colorado took the entire myColorado app offline after its Office of Information Technology (OIT) found an unauthorized user in the app's platform data on the night of Nov. 18, 2026, per [OIT's official statement](https://oit.colorado.gov/press-releases/mycolorado-mobile-app-temporarily-unavailable-office-of-information-technology). The state estimates 50,000 to 150,000 of about 2.1 million users may be affected. It has not said what data was involved or how the intruder got in. As of Nov. 25, the app is still down, with no return date. If you fly this Thanksgiving, carry a physical ID. Treat any "your ID app was breached" message as a lure until you confirm it on an official state site.

The myColorado app put a driver license, vehicle services, and other state services in one place. The convenience has a cost. When the app went dark, every one of those things went dark with it, right before Thanksgiving travel.

This post covers what the state has confirmed, what it has not, what travelers should carry, how to spot the scams a breach tends to attract, and why a digital ID needs a physical backup.

## What happened to the myColorado app? {#what-happened}

> Colorado's OIT found an unauthorized user in myColorado platform data on the night of Nov. 18, 2026. The state shut down the whole app to protect users' data, and the app remains offline while OIT investigates.

According to [OIT's Nov. 23 statement](https://oit.colorado.gov/press-releases/mycolorado-mobile-app-temporarily-unavailable-office-of-information-technology), the office "found a cybersecurity incident within the myColorado app where an unauthorized user was able to access platform data." OIT then "quickly shut down the entire app to protect Coloradans' data and began investigating the incident."

myColorado is the state's official app. It holds the Colorado Digital ID and the myColorado Mobile Driver License (mDL), a digital version of your physical license stored on your phone. It also links to services from the Division of Motor Vehicles and other agencies.

The [myColorado outage page](https://mycolorado.gov/), last updated Nov. 24, says teams are working "to investigate the cause and scope of the incident, fix the vulnerability, and get myColorado back online as soon as possible once appropriate protection measures are in place." It adds: "We don't have any reason to believe that this is a part of a bigger attack."

State CIO Sarah Tuneberg said in the statement: "We know how much Coloradans rely on these services, and we understand how this is frustrating and inconvenient. Protecting our users' data is our first priority, and we will bring systems back online as quickly as we can once security is restored."

The state announced the incident on Monday, Nov. 23, five days after finding it, as [9NEWS reported](https://www.9news.com/article/money/consumer/mycolorado-app-remains-offline-state-leaves-questions-about-delay-notifying-public-unanswered/73-4b025c7e-c064-4906-8efc-a9c9e71be55c). [The Record](https://therecord.media/mycolorado-app-down-data-breach) and [The Colorado Sun](https://coloradosun.com/2026/11/24/mycolorado-digital-id-app-down-cyber-security-incident/) confirmed the app was still offline on Tuesday, Nov. 24.

## How many myColorado app users may be affected? {#who-is-affected}

> OIT estimates the incident may have impacted the data of 50,000 to 150,000 people. The app has about 2.1 million users. The state calls this an estimate and says the investigation is ongoing.

The range comes from OIT itself. Even the high end works out to about 7% of users, and the state has not said who falls inside it.

OIT says: "Once that investigation is completed, anyone whose data is impacted will be specifically notified with information on any steps they can take to limit impacts from the disclosure." Colorado law gives government agencies up to 30 days after determining a breach to notify affected people, per 9NEWS.

Some headlines go further than the state. The Record's headline says the state "warns 150,000 had data stolen." OIT's own wording is "may have impacted between 50,000-150,000 individuals' data." This post uses the state's wording.

If you want context on what a good breach notice looks like, our breakdown of [the Boston Health Care for the Homeless breach notification](/2026/08/12/data-breach-notification-184914-homeless/) covers the questions every notice should answer.

## How do you get through TSA without the myColorado app? {#travel}

> Bring a physical, TSA-acceptable ID, such as a REAL ID-compliant driver license or a U.S. passport. If you prefer a phone ID, Colorado supports other DMV-approved digital wallets. TSA ConfirmID costs $45 and offers no guarantee.

The state's answer is direct. The [myColorado outage page](https://mycolorado.gov/) says: "For your upcoming travel, please use your physical driver license or state-issued ID card for identity verification at the airport."

Three facts from TSA's own pages matter here:

1. **TSA expects a physical ID even with a digital one.** TSA's [digital ID page](https://www.tsa.gov/digital-id) says "all passengers must still carry an acceptable compliant physical ID for verification."
2. **Several IDs qualify.** [TSA's acceptable ID list](https://www.tsa.gov/travel/security-screening/identification) includes REAL ID-compliant state licenses and ID cards, U.S. passports, passport cards, and trusted traveler cards such as Global Entry. TSA does not require ID from children under 18 on domestic trips.
3. **Colorado has other digital options.** TSA's [participating states list](https://www.tsa.gov/digital-id/participating-states) shows Colorado IDs in Apple Wallet, Google Wallet, and Samsung Wallet. OIT says myColorado users "can choose to add their mDL to another DMV-approved digital wallet," and the [Colorado DMV](https://dmv.colorado.gov/colorado-id-on-your-phone) explains how.

If you have no acceptable ID at all, [TSA ConfirmID](https://www.tsa.gov/tsaconfirm-id) is a fallback. You pay a $45 fee through Pay.gov, and TSA "will then attempt to verify your identity." TSA states "there is no guarantee TSA can do so," and the fee is non-refundable. Leave extra time if you go this route.

One more detail from the state: some people still see their Digital ID in airplane mode, because myColorado caches data on the phone. The state says some data "may not be cached" if you have not opened it recently or reinstalled the app. Do not plan a trip around a cached screen.

## Why does a digital ID need a physical backup? {#backup}

> A digital ID depends on an app, a server, a phone, and a battery. If any one fails, your ID fails with it. A physical card has none of those dependencies.

A physical ID works like a spare house key: you rarely think about it until the day you are locked out and nothing else opens the door.

This is the resilience point, and it matters more than the breach itself for most readers. A single app holding your ID, your vehicle records, and your state services puts many eggs in one basket. A phone with a dead battery, a cracked screen, or a forced shutdown like this one takes all of them away at once.

None of this makes digital IDs unsafe. TSA calls mobile driver licenses "more secure than physical credentials, which can be counterfeit or fraudulent," and says they share only the information TSA needs. The problem is not the format but having no plan B.

Colorado already builds the backup into the rules. The outage page notes: "Colorado law requires you to carry your physical ID at all times, even if choosing to use a digital credential." Use the phone version for convenience. Keep the card for when the phone or the app lets you down.

## How do you spot myColorado breach scams? {#phishing}

> Treat any unexpected text, email, or call about the breach as a lure. Do not tap its links or call its numbers. Confirm on myColorado.gov, which the state names as its update channel.

Breach news gives scammers a script. Expect messages like "Your myColorado data was exposed, verify your identity here" or "Pay a fee to restore your digital ID." Phishing means a fake message built to trick you into handing over information or money. Smishing is the same trick by text.

The state has said it will notify affected people after its investigation ends. It has not announced any notices yet. So a message today claiming to confirm your exposure deserves extra suspicion.

Use these checks:

- **Go to the source yourself.** Type myColorado.gov or oit.colorado.gov into your browser. Do not use a link from the message.
- **Never pay to "restore" an ID.** Colorado's [Protect Yourself Online page](https://oit.colorado.gov/protect-yourself-online) states: "Government agencies never demand payment by gift card, cryptocurrency or wire transfer."
- **Do not share codes.** No real notice needs your password or a one-time login code.
- **Report it.** The state page directs people to report government impersonation scams to the [FTC](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/) and suspicious messages claiming to be from a state agency to isoc@state.co.us.

Travel week adds a second wave of lures, such as fake hotel or airline payment requests. Our post on [the hotel booking scam](/2026/11/12/hotel-booking-scam-shared-login-whiteboard/) shows how to check a payment request against your real booking.

## What should myColorado app users do now? {#checklist}

> Carry your physical ID, watch for scams, and wait for official notice before you act on a claim about your data. If you reused your myColorado password elsewhere, change it there now.

A short checklist for anyone using a state digital ID or a mobile wallet ID:

1. **Pack the physical card.** Check it is REAL ID-compliant or bring a passport. Do this before you leave for the airport.
2. **Set up a second option if you want one.** Add your Colorado ID to a DMV-approved wallet through the [Colorado DMV](https://dmv.colorado.gov/colorado-id-on-your-phone). It is optional, not required.
3. **Use agency sites while the app is down.** The state points people to [myDMV](https://mydmv.colorado.gov/_/) for license and registration renewals, and to other agency portals.
4. **Fix reused passwords.** The state has not said whether passwords were involved. If your myColorado password works on any other account, change the password on each of those accounts and turn on multi-factor authentication (a second login step, such as a code or app prompt).
5. **Consider a credit freeze.** A freeze blocks new credit in your name and costs nothing. It is a calm, reversible step. Our [credit freeze guide](/2026/02/26/lexisnexis-credit-freezes-your-guide-to-identity-protection/) walks through it.
6. **Watch for the real notice.** When it arrives, compare it to what the state posts on myColorado.gov before you follow any steps in it.

If someone already uses your identity, [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/) from the FTC builds a recovery plan for your situation. ## What should teams building digital ID apps take from this? {#builders}

> Plan for the day the app goes offline. Every service the app fronts needs a working path without it, and users need a clear backup before an outage, not during one.

Colorado had working fallbacks. Agency websites still served people, and other wallets still held the mDL. Even so, the state had to ask travelers to switch to a physical card during Thanksgiving week.

If you run a service people rely on for identity:

- **Document the fallback for every feature** and test it, so a shutdown never strands users.
- **Tell users their backup options during onboarding**, not in an outage notice.
- **Publish a single, known update channel** so users have one place to confirm news and spot a scam.

Shutting down a whole app is a hard call, because it protects data at the cost of availability. Good design makes the cost smaller.

## The bottom line

Colorado shut down the myColorado app after finding an unauthorized user in its data. Up to 150,000 people may be affected, and the state has not said what was involved. Until it does, stay calm and stay skeptical.

Keep your physical ID with you. Treat breach messages as lures until the state confirms them. Use the phone version of your ID as a convenience and the card as your backup.
