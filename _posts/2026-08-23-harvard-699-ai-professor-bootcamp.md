A bootcamp costing Harvard almost nothing to repeat still charges every founder full price.

**TL;DR:** Harvard Business School's $699 HBS Foundry bootcamp lets founders rehearse pitches with AI video avatars of its own professors, built by HeyGen. Project director Katharina Rings recorded each avatar once. Every student since has practiced with the same recording at no added production cost. Harvard sits on a $56.9 billion endowment and already gives entire courses away for free through its Professional and Lifelong Learning catalog. The math behind this price does not hold up, and Harvard has the resources to fix it without losing a dollar.

---

HBS Foundry is Harvard Business School's newest bootcamp for entrepreneurs. It runs eight weeks, costs $699, and pairs live sessions with founders, investors, and HBS faculty against an AI-powered portal built to fill the gaps between those sessions. The portal's signature feature is a set of AI video avatars, built by HeyGen, standing in for real HBS instructors during pitch rehearsals and mock board meetings. A student pitching to one of these avatars gets the same experience as the student before them and the student after them. Harvard recorded the professor once. It gets reused forever.

## What does HBS Foundry include for $699? {#what-is-foundry}

> **Direct answer:** HBS Foundry is an eight-week, $699 online bootcamp from Harvard Business School. It pairs weekly live sessions with investors and faculty with an AI portal built around HeyGen video avatars of instructors for pitch practice.

[Foundry director Katharina Rings](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/22/harvards-699-startup-bootcamp-offers-ai-avatars-of-its-instructors/) originally planned a simple chatbot for the AI side of the program. Early testers pushed back. They wanted a guided, structured practice session, not a text box. Her team built full video-call simulations instead, using avatar technology from HeyGen. Founders now open the portal, pick an instructor, and run through a pitch, a valuation defense, or a tough board question, on demand, at any hour.

This kind of AI-driven, on-demand coaching is spreading across higher education. We covered the wider shift in [our guide to which colleges now offer dedicated AI degrees](/2026/07/06/do-colleges-offer-ai-degrees-2026-guide/).

## How much does an AI avatar cost Harvard to run? {#marginal-cost}

> **Direct answer:** Almost nothing per student. Harvard pays once to record and train each avatar. After the recording, HeyGen's platform generates the video responses on demand, at a marginal cost close to the price of a cloud compute call, not a professor's hourly rate.

Compare the two cost structures side by side.

| Resource | One-time cost | Cost per extra student |
|---|---|---|
| A real HBS professor holding office hours | Salary, always | Roughly a full hour of the professor's time |
| An AI avatar of the same professor | Recording and training session, once | A few cents of cloud inference, repeated forever |

A professor's hour is a fixed, scarce resource. A trained AI avatar is not. Once HeyGen renders the model, Harvard sells the same fifteen-minute pitch rehearsal to one student or to ten thousand students, and the cost barely moves.

The same math applies to plenty of paid certifications and bootcamps outside Harvard. We ran the numbers on one of the most popular ones in [our review of whether the Google Cybersecurity Certificate is worth the price](/2026/06/25/google-cybersecurity-certification-review/), and the pattern holds: once the content is recorded, the marginal cost of another enrollment drops close to zero.

## Why does the price stay fixed at $699 if the cost keeps falling? {#the-real-math}

> **Direct answer:** Harvard is pricing AI mentorship like a scarce, staffed resource, even though it is not one anymore. The school holds a $56.9 billion endowment and already distributes free coursework elsewhere, so the $699 price reflects positioning, not necessity.

Harvard Management Company reported an endowment worth <a href="https://finance.harvard.edu/fy25-hmc-letter">$56.9 billion</a> as of June 30, 2025, up from $53.2 billion the year before. The endowment distributed <a href="https://finance.harvard.edu/endowment">$2.5 billion</a> to the university in fiscal year 2025 alone, more than a third of total operating revenue.

Harvard also knows how to give things away. Its <a href="https://pll.harvard.edu/catalog/free">Professional and Lifelong Learning catalog</a> lists free courses across the humanities, sciences, and business. CS50, its flagship computer science course, has run as a free audit on edX for over a decade and turned into one of the most recognized names in online education.

So HBS Foundry did not need a $699 price tag to exist. Someone chose $699 anyway, for a product whose most expensive line item, the AI avatar, gets cheaper with every additional sign-up. This pricing decision protects the exclusivity of the Harvard name more than it protects Harvard's budget.

## What do people who used the AI avatars say? {#what-people-say}

> **Direct answer:** Reactions are split. HBS senior lecturer Jeff Bussgang called his own AI avatar creepy, then said his students love it. A New York Times reporter who tested it described a frozen, unconvincing smile.

[New York Times reporter Sarah Kessler](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/22/business/dealbook/harvard-ai-faculty.html) pitched an idea for an app she called Uber for bananas to an AI clone of Jeff Bussgang, the Flybridge Capital co-founder and HBS senior lecturer whose likeness Foundry uses for pitch practice. Neither Bussgang nor his AI double was impressed. Kessler noted the avatar answered with a noticeably frozen smile.

Bussgang has not hidden the eeriness of watching his own digital copy field questions from strangers all day. "My students love it," he said, then called the digital version of himself creepy in the same breath.

Both reactions hold at the same time. Feedback which once required booking time with a busy investor is now available on demand, any hour, to any student in the program. Harvard still charges as though the old scheduling constraint were real.

## What should Harvard do instead? {#what-harvard-should-do}

> **Direct answer:** Harvard has room to lower the price, add a free tier for its own students and alumni, or fold the AI avatars into existing free offerings like its Professional and Lifelong Learning catalog, without losing money on the technology itself.

None of this means HBS Foundry should shut down. Founders are getting something real: structured practice, a deadline, and feedback from a system modeled on people who built and funded real companies. The problem sits in the pricing, not the product.

A few fixes cost Harvard little and fit what the school already does elsewhere.

- Offer free or discounted AI avatar access to current HBS students and recent alumni first, before selling seats to the general public.
- Publish the marginal cost of running an avatar session, the way HeyGen and similar vendors already publish per-minute compute pricing, so the $699 figure has to compete with a real number.
- Fold a limited version of the avatar practice tool into the existing free Professional and Lifelong Learning catalog, which already hosts CS50 for free.

Harvard did not build a cheaper way to teach founders by accident. It built a tool whose cost per student keeps falling as more students sign up. Pricing it like scarce office hours protects a number, not a mission.
